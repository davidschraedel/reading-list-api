Notes: Halo effect: we see one attractive trait as evidence for the existence of others that we haven’t observed. Natural selection favored a paranoid mindset. Proportionality bias: We look for perfect ratios, we look for explanations. The greater the effect, the greater we desire the cause to be. Social media influencers foster reliance, instead of teaching you to think for yourself. Frequency bias, confirmation bias, and proportionality bias, create manifestation and law of attraction thinking. Frequency bias can lead to over-diagnosis simply because we heard about a condition recently. A toxic relationship is just a cult of one. Let those in a cult relationship know that if they ever want to get out, even just chat, you will be there (you won’t judge, interrogate, shame, etc.). They may not talk to you for a while, but they won’t forget. Sometimes it needs to be said so that they know that others see them suffering, and it’s not too late to cut their losses. Sunk cost fallacy: We evolved to avoid defeat at all costs. When we find ourselves in a losing situation, we tend to persevere, telling ourselves it will work out. We don’t want to be seen as having made a poor choice. In nature, we use the sunk cost fallacy to cast ourselves in a socially positive light. We don’t want to admit we made a bad bet. Honoring sunk costs may make us seem more consistent, like we know ourselves well, and can be trusted. To appear socially valuable, we feel pressure to appear the best we can. We want to believe the fairytale version of us as much as we want to share it with others. We have a bias toward additive solutions. We rarely think to take something away. We are biased toward adding new things to solve problems, and forget that maybe we don’t need what we have, and removing this may solve the problem. We have a capacity for attention, and the amount of information we are exposed to exceeds that capacity. Zero-sum fallacy: the false assumption that another party’s gain means your loss. Whenever someone profits from exchange, we assume the other person was ripped off, yet that is not true. People rarely pay for something they don’t value as much as they spend on it. Some things, as in the past, are zero-sum, like how much pizza you can each get from one pie. Yet, win-win scenarios are the entire reason we accomplish more in groups. Our monetary system has made this extremely abstract, so we find it more difficult to decide value measurements. It is also difficult to tell who is in our social circle, and who we deem our competitors. Our “worst competitors” usually remind us of ourselves. They may be the people we may click with best. In the eastern world, places like Japan, people are more likely to be happy playing a small role in a prestigious company. They are fine being a small fish in a big pond.  Social media inflates social comparison, and imposter syndrome. We don’t know who to compare ourselves to, so we compare against everyone's fairytale life. These people are also likely great collaborators, but we think their wins mean our loss.  Women tend to compare themselves with their “superiors”, and men with their “inferiors”. Girls therefore learn in middle school to see the “threats” around them. Capitalism + feminism is classic zero-sum bias fuel. Women “collaborate” with other women only to tear them down later. Tokenizing members of marginalized communities promotes a culture of noxious infighting. To adopt tokenism, you have to sell your community out: instead of blaming institutions and systems around you for your problems, you end up blaming your community. We spend time frustrated with those we think of as “in our circle” in some way. Essentially we are practicing catharsis. Catharsis is proven to be a false notion, because we don’t gain less of something by practicing it. Yelling and screaming doesn’take us less angry. It does the opposite, deepening those pathways in our mind. Snorting drugs makes us want too do it more. Trash talking just makes us better at trash talking. When you disparage someone behind their back, spontaneous trait transference occurs, where you start to assume the qualities you assign to the subject of discussion. Shine theory: surrounding yourself with talented people doesn’t make you look worse, it makes you look better. Connect, don’t reject. Survivorship bias: propensity to focus on positive outcomes or results, ignoring misfortunes or negative outcomes in our analysis.  For example, we focus on the few successful college dropouts and mistakenly assume that is the path of success. We often don’t know which planes fly the worst, because those planes don't come back. We disregard invisible failures, selectively attending to the one successful role model, only what we observe as passing the test. We often want our life to end up like a scripted narrative. Lacking optimism is essentially lacking agency. We wake up and do the same thing again, feeling that we can’t change it. Inertia is cozy, but trying something new comes with uncertainty that can inspire hope.  Recency bias: We think something is new or recent or just happened, only because we just learned about it (it is new to only us). New things feel more serious. We tend to move on quickly from things now because  it is difficult to tell what is worth our focus in an oversaturated stimuli environment. Current marketing targets the amygdala. The recency bias is the limbic system, your amygdala trying to help you stay safe. Excessive phone use actually decreases dopamine hits to real life events. We essentially desensitize ourselves to real-world stimuli and the natural drive we should have toward them. Digital slot-machine novelty inhibits our ability to enjoy the real world. The amount of information available has caused our attention span to shrink. We are experiencing cognitive exhaustion. What is new also slows time,  making our childhood seem longer because everything was new to us. A new relationship feels similar.  Covid lockdown was the opposite experience, because we had so few experiences worth recording in our memory. Mindfulness is essentially seeing things anew, because it is new or has changed, and is proven to help with stress, etc. Overconfidence bias: overvalue their skills, express excessive certainty in their evaluatios, and overcredit themselves with positive outcomes. The more murder documentaries we consume, the more we feel we could handle it. A false sense of security.  Untalented criminals and silicon Valley ceos.  We all tend to overestimate their moral compass, everyday skills (driving), and common knowledge. Depressed people are more likely to assess their talents more objectively than others, “depressive realism”. The more dopamine you have, the less connection between frontal lobe and striatum, the less objectively and realistically you judge yourself. Those with lots of dopamine activity are living blissfully unaware, in the “mentally healthy” state. Overconfidence bias is the root of failing to copy those pinterest cupcakes, even though we thought we could easily. Overconfident parties enter more wars, expanding quickly, but success blinds them to failure. The most adored figures in our society are the most confident or arrogant.  In 2022, 98% of middle and high school students wanted to be internet famous, according to blooomberg. Bluffer is valued. If you really belive it, it perpetuates the cycle, as long as it’s not too unrealistic, people believe you because you believe yourself. Perhaps none is more dangerous and problematic than our overconfidence bias. We sentence people to life in prison or death sentence in error. Imposter syndrome: a workplace induced anxiety disorder. Does anyone not feel it? No. Imposter syndrome my not even exist, but simply be a symptom of the system. Overconfidence is a mental posture. The average person is quick to turn defensive. Women are more morally punishing, and display more higher self-conscious moral emotions and empathic concern. Most women consider poor behavior more morally wrong, and deserving of harsher punishment. The moral reproach is another side of the overconfidence bias, though. It attributes positive outcomes to ourself, and blames others for negative results. There are incentives now to critique others, even from a place of privilege or hypocrisy. It’s the people who almost live in glass houses, who decide to cast the first stone. We overconfidently assume we know more than we do, because of what others know. We think we know how a zipper works, but we can’think really say. Someone knows, but it’s not us. We have so much “knowledge”, it’so hard to say where theirs ends and ours begins.  The Google effect: we forget what we search quickly (it doesn’t become our knowledge), and we forget that we forgot it. Gaps in understanding drive us forward. Humility: a low focus on the self; an accurate, not over or underestimated sense of one’s accomplishments and and worth; an acknowledgement of one's  limitations, imperfections, mistakes, gaps in knowledge, etc. Worry about others’ judging you is more self focus. Our culture endorses self deceptive bluffing. All we really want is to be present, connect,and show up for life. Reward people for admitting their mistakes. The winners are never overly confident or overly humble. Their is a nuanced level, even a biased one, with a little bit of illusion. But, even better, treat life less like a war. Embrace the ordinary, and the fact that we don’t need to be exceptional.  The illusory truth effect: our penchant to trust a truth as factual, simply because we’ve heard it multiple times. Propoganda spreads this way. Masters of the bias can become tyrants. When people lack knowledge, literacy, or motivation to critically evaluate a message, they rely on heuristics, like “familiar sayings tend to be credible”. Your brain misinterprets fluency for accuracy. the more we encounter a claim in the wild, the more difficult it becomes to dismiss it. Repetition may be the closest thing we have to a magical spell. Society's most repeated proverbs are not necessarily true, but the ones that make the most cultural sense. They make opposing tropes more difficult to hear or unlikely to be heard. Rhyme is privileged mentally, because it is predictable, or patterned, making it more sensible, and we find this pleasurable. When an utterance is more pleasurable, we also find it to be more trustworthy. Jokes allow people to share problematic ideas, similarly. Political dogmas become such because they are repeated with fervor. When it sounds good, that’s a cue to be wary. Knowledge isn’t always meant to go down like a nursery rhyme.   We experience the illusory truth effect internally as well. The more we hear a story from ourself, the more we believe it. Confirmation bias: we tend to favor information that validates our existing views, and discard that which refutes them. It helps you work backwards to find the right “clues” to confirm your existing views. The prefrontal cortex plugs its ears. We ignore the wealth of evidence that suggests we should change our perspective. This is one reason more information doesn’t calm our doomsday fears. There are still people who thought the world would end. Astrology is used as an excuse to explain behavior. Confirmations bias serves to help bring social reality into alignment with our beliefs.  It helps us avoid agonizing over every choice. It helps you get rid of the tiny uncertainties to be able to make tiny decisions. More information and scientific awareness leads people to become more skillfull at picking out the details that support their beliefs. It means they have more to choose from to confirm their view.  People are most likely to change their own mind, not others. We idealized the past. We are progressing, more comfortable, etc., but not happier. 30% of people consider themselves extremely happy. We experience Anemoia. Past events exist only in memory, how we create them. The future is unknown, so we look to the “familiar” in the past. Fading effect bias: bad memories fade faster than good memories. Hitler used the slogan “make Germany great again”, just like “make america great again” used by Ronald Reagan, Bill clinton, George w bush, and  Donald trump. Politicians use the glorified past, the nostalgia for the “good old days”, to achieve their agenda and win votes, while brushing the trouble of the past under the carpet.  Many feminists oddly enough turned trad wives, perhaps because they felt their promised success didn’t pan out. Negativity bias: tendency to assign greater weight to unfavorable events. Consumerism is our culturally induced coping mechanism. For creativity, give your brain a break from consuming. It’s hard to consume and create at the same time. We like things we build more than superior items we don’t build. Adding an egg makes the cake mix “your own”. We need a feeling that we caused something to happen in the world. We need contribution, even if it’s just an egg. Jobs are our least desirable, but must satisfying activities in the modern individualist world. We rationalize the value of things we spend effort on.
