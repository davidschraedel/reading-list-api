# You are not so smart

Author: David McRaney

Notes: Just about every physical object you encounter triggers a blitz of associations throughout your mind. You construct reality from minute to minute with memories and emotions orbiting your sensations and cognition. (You are walking memory, and make meaning based on perception and the relevant memory and emotion). Smell is a great example of this. We like and dislike certain smells, but many smells are neutral, and only perceived one way or another based on previous experience, or memory and emotion, what we think we remember. This sort of priming or influence with our action or perception works best when we are on autopilot and not consciously trying to introspect. Your brain hates ambiguity, and is willing to take shortcuts to remove it. It uses what is available. If pattern recognition fails, your create patterns of your own. You also chunk perceptions all the time to reduce complexity. Written language is valuable because it escapes the rational mind and its limits. The emotional brain is better at breakdancing, somersaults and singing because these actions require too many steps and variables for the rational mind to handle. You hand these tasks over to the adaptive unconscious. Animals with small cerebral cortices, or none, are mostly on autopilot because their emotional brain runs the show. We mistakenly believe our rational mind is in control, oblivious to the influence of our subconscious. We are comfortable with top-down control (hugging and then feeling endorphins),  but find bottom-up control odd or uncomfortable (simply observing a briefcase makes you act more greedily than you otherwise would, taking advise from the hidden advisors of emotion and subconscious). From every corner of the modern world, advertisers are launching attacks on your unconscious to prime your behavior to be more favorable to their bottom lines. We believe the things around us don’t effect us. Much of what you think, feel, do, believe is, and will continue to be, nudged by primes from words, colors, objects, smells, personalities, etc. That are infused with meaning via your life or cultural experience. Make plans in advance to be primed, such as making a grocery list that keeps you on track and avoids autopilot roaming the store. You can't prime yourself directly, but you can create environments conducive to mental states you desire. Choose to be an agent and prime others: smile, dress appropriately, etc. When you least expect it, your environment will nudge you. Your brain fills in gaps, blindspots in your memory, reason, and perception. The details are missing, but the big picture persists, which is often a lie  to a degree, because the left and right brain send signals to each other and undoubtedly miscommunicate.. they often must invent reasons for why they receive a signal, and without the connections biologically between the two brain halves, they would make up explanations or be at a loss for reason all of the time. The left hemisphere often leads the dance because it deals with talking. You are a confabulatory creature by nature, and create reasons for the causes and motivations in your life. Over time, this becomes your idea of who you are and your place in the world, or your “self”. Your sense of self is partly your mirror neurons, which fire when you see others experience things, but also when you act, as if part of your brain observes you from the outside. “You” is a story you tell yourself, a confabulation that without which you could not function. When you recall the past, you create it on the spot, using part memory and part fantasy to recreate memory which you believe fully. We don't believe our daydreams usually. But believe our inaccurate recollection. We are unaware of how our mind works, of how we come to decisions and feelings. We merely make up stories. Ask yourself to recall your mother’s maiden name, and you will have no clue how you truly remembered it, you just “thought”. In many ways, you only report on what your mind has 
already produced, instead of directing the production. Phenomenology. Consciousness and recollection of it are separate, but we often think of them as one. Heterophenomenology. Take your own perception with a grain of salt. We seldom simply say we don't know why we do what we do. We almost always 
find a way to justify our actions in some way. Who you think you are is like a movie based on true events. It's embellished, but tye bigger picture is there and still worth hearing about. You can't ever know the objective truth, but that's OK. Confirmation bias: seeing the world through a filter, partly due to seeing top-of-mind topics everywhere. People like to be told what they already know, to watch for confirmation, not information. People think they crave news, but they really crave olds. You want to be right about how you see the world, so you seek to confirm your views, not contradict them. Even recalling memory falls prey to this, meaning memory can be rewritten unconsciously to fit our biases. Remember there's always someone willing to sell an audience looking for validation. Are you part of that audience? Hindsight bias: we assume we new or believed it all along. When you learn something new, you quickly redact your past so you can feel the comfort of always being right. What we usually think of as common sense, usually isn't. It's more likely hindsight bias reconstructing our past views to fit current settings. We likewise delete old info about ourselves when we change, because we don't need the clutter anymore. We remember ourselves as being more accurately inside with current circumstances, our past predictions as more accurate, our past follies as less problematic, than they are. Much like the availability heuristic, we tend to prioritize what is in front of us, and see the world through that lense, seeing it as more prevalent than it is, because that is what is there. We truly think reality is what is currently attended to, and rewrite the past to fit the present. We all think we were never wrong.  The Texas sharpshooter fallacy: you tend to ignore random chance when results seem meaningful or when you want random events to have meaningful cause. With meaning you overlook randomness, but meaning is a human construction. we paint a bull’s-eye over an already tattered cluster of bulletholes, making artificial order of natural chaos. Reality TV, news, etc., do this all day. They draw a target over a cluster of randomness to make a point, a story, to fit their agenda. Essentially, the sharpshooter fallacy is a way to find a target, an enemy, to deal with the anxiety of being at the whim of chance, and the way is to ignore Confounding variables and the bigger picture of random chance. It is humans finding meaning after the fact, after the numbers have been crunched, ignoring all the times the ball missed the basket, or the church was torn by a tornado, or the casino luck didn't pan out. Only the need for meaning changes how you feel about what you see. Creating this false order helped our ancestors survive.  We see patterns everywhere, but some of them are merely chance.  Procrastination: you have time-inconsistent preferences. You will plan for fruit, but take the cake instead on the actual day. In the moment, you go for what taste good, present-bias (unable to grasp that what you want will change over time, and what you want now isn’t what you’ll want later). Your will succumbs to death by a thousand cuts, as you make poor present choices over and over. (Thus, often, action precedes desire). You’re not a bad manager, you're a bad tactician inside your brain’s war. The marshmallow kids tricked themselves into getting what they would want later. They looked at the wall, tapped their feet, instead of staring at the marshmallow. Thinking about thinking, is the key. “Want” never goes away. Hyperbolic discounting. Believe that you will procrastinate or struggle, or else you never develop a strategy to cope with it and become overly idealistic about your capabikities. Now-you needs to convince future-you to make a different choice rather than giving in to old habit or temptation. This is why buying only good food is a better health plan then other alternatives, etc. Future-you has to deal with that choice in the fiture, but not make it initially. Normalcy bias: we tend to believe that things are normal, even in emergency. We will assume that it is someone else's problem. That we are not in danger. To the point we calm others down so we don't have to feel panicked, to reassure ourselves that things are still normal. Pretending evrryth8ng will continue to be fine Freeze state almost, and some enter freeze state. Those who act and survive are those who prepared, thought ahead, and rehearsed in their mind what they would do or practiced their strategy. Cognition, perception, comprehension, decision, implementation, and then movement. N9rmalcy bias is not freeze state. It is self-soothing. It is seeing the world as if nothing bad happened so you can feel safe. Refusing to beleive that terrible events will include you. Denial of yourself as part of the unnatural or nonnormal. In disaster you feel the need to feel safe. Survivors of 9 11 walked out of the building leisurely as if nothing wrong was happening, calling loved ones and gathering belongings on their way. You will cling to normalcy, to what you know. Then you will mine others for information. First you will interpret based on what you know, likely severely underestimate yhe threat, seek information from those you trust, then those around you, then contact family, then prepare to evacuate . Introspection: origin of your feelings is not clear to you, even though you make up reasons and justifications.
